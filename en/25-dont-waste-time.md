# 25. Don't waste time

<!-- zh-intro: ce01ef8d -->

This section only counts time. It looks at how many hours a day, week or year you can win back, or how much the proportion of things finished on time rises. Time is not converted into lifespan or money here. Where money is involved, only the method of calculation is given, not a conclusion. Most entries in this section rest on a single study or on aggregate statistics from time-use surveys, so grades are mainly B and C.

This section carries over the original book's section "don't waste time", with the research figures unchanged. The Chinese time-use survey used in the original book has been replaced by the equivalent statistics from Japan's Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications.

### 1. Turn "I plan to do it" into "at what time, where, and on what cue I'll do what"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 把「打算做」写成「几点、在哪、遇到什么就做什么」 | 308e151f -->
- Cost: Free. Spend 1 to 2 minutes per task writing an "if..., then..." sentence. For example, "On Tuesday at 8 a.m., when I sit down at my desk, I'll start by writing the introduction".
- In plain words: Rewrite "I'm going to do this" as "On Tuesday at 8 a.m., when I sit down at my desk, I'll start by writing the introduction". Pooling 94 tests, people who wrote it this way achieved their goals clearly more often, a medium-to-large effect. All that changes is how one sentence is written; it costs no money and no effort.
- Benefit: Pooling 94 independent tests, people who first wrote their plans as "if..., then..." achieved their goals more often. In psychology this way of writing is called an implementation intention. The effect size was d = 0.65, medium to large. It helps at three points: getting started, holding out when interrupted, and pulling out of things doomed to fail.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>
- Notes: This only works for things you genuinely want to do. If you do not want the goal itself, writing more sentences will not help. Also, these studies mostly involved students and mostly looked at health habits, so it may not work as well in work settings.

### 2. Estimate how long something will take from how long similar tasks actually took, not from your plan
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 估工期时按过去同类任务的实际耗时估，不按计划推 | 197e3b51 -->
- Cost: Free. Before estimating, spend a few minutes looking up how long a similar task actually took last time.
- In plain words: Students estimated they would finish their theses in 33.9 days, but took 55.5 days, and only 29.7% finished within their own estimate. Another group was asked first to recall how long a similar task took last time before estimating, and the proportion finishing on time rose from 29.3% to 60.0%.
- Benefit: Students expected to finish their theses in 33.9 days but actually took 55.5 days. Only 29.7% finished on time by their own estimate. The researchers asked another group to first link past experience to the task at hand, then estimate. In this group, the proportion finishing on time rose from the control group's 29.3% to 60.0%. The optimism that underestimates how long things take no longer appeared in this group.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67(3), 366–381. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366>；Flyvbjerg, B. (2006). From Nobel Prize to project management: Getting risks right. Project Management Journal, 37(3), 5–15. <https://doi.org/10.1177/875697280603700302>；Halkjelsvik, T., & Jørgensen, M. (2012). From origami to software development: A review of studies on judgment-based predictions of performance time. Psychological Bulletin, 138(2), 238–271. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025996>
- Notes: The Buehler study only involved Canadian university students, is a single study, and had few people: 37, and about 40 per group. Estimating from historical data on similar projects is called reference class forecasting; for how it is done in engineering, see Flyvbjerg. Halkjelsvik, pooling many studies, points out that underestimating is more common than overestimating, but by how much varies greatly with the task and research method.

### 3. Write down your exit conditions before you start
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 开工前写下退出条件 | 0d876b05 -->
- Cost: Free. When starting, spend 5 minutes writing clearly "if a certain target is not met by a certain date, stop". The hard part is accepting it when the day comes; stopping in front of others is a bit embarrassing.
- In plain words: Before starting, write one sentence: if a certain target has not been met by a certain date, stop. Pooling several studies, writing it this way genuinely helps people pull out of things doomed to fail. What you save is all the time you would otherwise have kept pouring in.
- Benefit: Pooling several implementation intention studies: pre-set "if..., then..." plans likewise help people pull out of things doomed to fail. The original wording is disengagement from failing courses of action. The time saved is what you would otherwise have kept investing.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>
- Notes: Exit conditions should be set at the start, while you are still calm. Set afterwards, they are skewed by what you have already invested. When deciding, look only at future investment and future returns; see entry 15 (look only at future investment and returns). Also, "willingness to pull out" is just one sub-finding in a pooled analysis, and no one has calculated separately how much time it saves.

### 4. Cut the number of meetings; do not meet about things that can be settled asynchronously in writing
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 砍会议数量，能用文字异步解决的不开会 | daab8b13 -->
- Cost: Free. You need colleagues' and your boss's cooperation. The hard part is that writing a clear paragraph takes more effort than saying a sentence.
- In plain words: The more meetings people have on a day, the more tired they are and the more work they feel they have. Cut one meeting and you save exactly that meeting's length, not a minute less.
- Benefit: Two surveys of employees working 35 or more hours a week, with 676 and 304 people. How much meeting time affected work attitudes and well-being was not fixed, depending on two things: how good the meetings were, and how much the job depends on others. Another study had people keep diaries for a week; the more meetings that day, the higher their fatigue and perceived workload. The time saved equals the length of the meetings you cut.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Rogelberg, S. G., Leach, D. J., Warr, P. B., & Burnfield, J. L. (2006). "Not another meeting!" Are meeting time demands related to employee well-being? Journal of Applied Psychology, 91(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.91.1.83>；Luong, A., & Rogelberg, S. G. (2005). Meetings and more meetings: The relationship between meeting load and the daily well-being of employees. Group Dynamics: Theory, Research, and Practice, 9(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2699.9.1.58>
- Notes: Disputed: Rogelberg 2006 found the relationship between number of meetings and well-being is not one-sided. In jobs highly dependent on others, many meetings are not necessarily bad. Also, no study has directly compared "switching to asynchronous written communication" with meeting; this entry is the author's own inference.

### 5. Set your own date for things with no external deadline
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 给没有外部截止的事自己定一个日期 | 6458f0f9 -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is committing once it is set; if you ignore it when the day comes, it is as if you never set it.
- In plain words: With a deadline set by someone else, 80.6% of students finished before it. Only 38.7% finished within their own estimate. When things get finished is almost entirely decided by the deadline, with little relation to one's own estimate. So for things without a deadline, set one yourself, and stick to it.
- Benefit: Of students with an external deadline, 80.6% finished before it. Only 38.7% finished within their own estimated time. When things were finished followed the deadline almost exactly, correlation coefficient r = 0.82; the closer this number is to 1, the closer it follows. It was only weakly related to their own estimates.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67(3), 366–381. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366>
- Notes: Telling others about your own date makes it closer to one set by someone else. Note that the research verified that deadlines set by others work. Self-set ones, such as time boxes and the Pomodoro technique, have not been tested directly; this is an extrapolation.

### 6. Do not watch TV or rolling news; check the information you need at set times
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 不看电视和滚动新闻，需要的信息定时集中看 | 0685d7ad -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is breaking the habit of switching it on casually, and you may miss some breaking news.
- In plain words: Americans aged 15 and over watch 2.6 hours of TV a day, half of all their leisure time. Japanese aged 10 and over spend an average of 2 hours 8 minutes a day on TV, radio, newspapers and magazines. Switching to checking the information you need at set times saves that whole block.
- Benefit: Americans aged 15 and over watch 2.6 hours of TV a day. Their total leisure time is 5.2 hours, so TV takes half. These are 2025 figures. In Japan's 2021 Survey on Time Use and Leisure Activities by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications, people aged 10 and over spent, averaged over a week, 2 hours 8 minutes a day on TV, radio, newspapers and magazines.
- Evidence: C
- Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (2026). American Time Use Survey — 2025 Results. <https://www.bls.gov/news.release/atus.nr0.htm>；総務省統計局 (2022). 令和３年社会生活基本調査 生活時間及び生活行動に関する結果 結果の概要. <https://www.stat.go.jp/data/shakai/2021/pdf/gaiyoua.pdf>
- Notes: There are only aggregate statistics. No study has calculated what the time saved by watching less TV and news is used for. "Checking at set times" is the author's experience.

### 7. After studying, close the book and test yourself instead of rereading
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 学完先合上书自己考一遍，别回头重读 | 287cc47d -->
- Cost: Free, and no extra total time. Use the time you would have spent rereading to recall from memory or do practice questions with the book closed. The hard part is that self-testing exposes what you have not remembered on the spot, which feels worse than rereading.
- In plain words: Rereading makes material feel familiar, but it does not stick. Closing the book and testing yourself works clearly better; adding quizzes in class raised grades by a medium amount. In a ranking of ten common study methods, self-testing was in the top tier. After reading a section, write out the key points from memory, then check against the book.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis of 222 independent studies with 48,478 students: classroom quizzes improved academic performance with a medium effect size (g = 0.499). The size of the effect depended on several conditions: what study method the control group used, whether the quiz format matched the final exam, whether the material matched, whether corrective feedback was given, how many times quizzes were repeated, where and when testing took place, how long the intervention lasted, and the experimental design. Another review assessing ten common learning techniques one by one rated "practice testing" and "distributed practice" in the top tier (high utility), because they work for learners of different ages and abilities and have been validated across many assessment formats and real educational settings.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Yang C, Luo L, Vadillo MA, Yu R, Shanks DR (2021). Testing (quizzing) boosts classroom learning: A systematic and meta-analytic review. Psychological Bulletin, 147(4), 399–435. <https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000309>；Dunlosky J, Rawson KA, Marsh EJ, Nathan MJ, Willingham DT (2013). Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques: Promising Directions From Cognitive and Educational Psychology. Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14(1), 4–58. <https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266>
- Notes: Self-testing is about forcing yourself to pull things out of memory, not about scoring. Method: after reading a section, close the book and write out the key points, then check against the book. It works better with corrective feedback, so after checking the answers, look carefully at what you got wrong. The benefit is rated large based on the effect size and rating. These two pieces of evidence measure grades, not hours, so the time-saved thresholds were not applied. Self-testing and entry 8 of this section (spread the same time over several days) are the two top-rated methods in the same review; use them together.

### 8. Spread the same study time over several days instead of doing it all at once
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 把同样的时间摊到几天，别一次学完 | 5f75477c -->
- Cost: Free, with the same total time. Only the scheduling changes: the same 3 hours split into 1 hour on each of three days. The hard part is starting several days in advance rather than leaving it to the last night.
- In plain words: The same amount of time spread over several days sticks better than studying all at once. 317 experiments tested this, with consistent conclusions. There is also a practical rule: the longer you need to remember something, the longer the gap between reviews should be. For a test tomorrow, review the same day; for a test in a month, go over it every few days.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis pooling 184 articles, 317 experiments and 839 assessments of distributed practice. It compared two things: studying in one go versus several sessions (the spacing effect), and short versus long gaps (the lag effect). It concluded that the review gap and "how long you need to remember" jointly determine retention on the final test: the longer the retention interval, the longer the review gap that gives the best retention. Another review assessing ten common learning techniques one by one rated distributed practice in the top tier (high utility).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Cepeda NJ, Pashler H, Vul E, Wixted JT, Rohrer D (2006). Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis. Psychological Bulletin, 132(3), 354–380. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354>；Dunlosky J, Rawson KA, Marsh EJ, Nathan MJ, Willingham DT (2013). Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques. Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14(1), 4–58. <https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266>
- Notes: Best combined with entry 7 of this section (close the book and test yourself): spread the self-testing itself over several days. The material in the Cepeda meta-analysis was mostly word-list memorization, and no general number of days is given for the best gap with complex content. "The further away the test, the longer the gap" is the rule given in the original, without a specific schedule. Cramming the night before an exam helps on the spot; the problem is you forget quickly.

### 9. Use your own hourly rate to decide which household chores to outsource
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=多 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 用自己的时薪决定哪些家务外包 | 643f65d6 -->
- Cost: Outsourcing costs money, depending on the service you buy. You also need to spend time once working out your hourly rate.
- In plain words: Japanese aged 10 and over spend an average of 1 hour 27 minutes a day on housework. People who spend money to buy time are more satisfied with their lives. The same money spent on buying time rather than things makes people happier at the time.
- Benefit: In Japan's 2021 Survey on Time Use and Leisure Activities by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications, people aged 10 and over did housework for 1 hour 27 minutes a day, averaged over a week. Another study sampled 6,271 people in four countries; those who spent money to buy time had higher life satisfaction. The researchers also ran a real-life experiment: the same money spent on buying time brought higher momentary happiness than spending it on material goods.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Whillans, A. V., Dunn, E. W., Smeets, P., Bekkers, R., & Norton, M. I. (2017). Buying time promotes happiness. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1706541114>；総務省統計局 (2022). 令和３年社会生活基本調査 生活時間及び生活行動に関する結果 結果の要約. <https://www.stat.go.jp/data/shakai/2021/pdf/youyakua.pdf>
- Notes: First work out your hourly rate: monthly take-home income ÷ actual working hours in a month. Then see what outsourcing the chore costs. If the outsourcing rate is below your hourly rate, and you will really use the saved time to earn or rest, outsource it. Everyone's hourly rate and outsourcing prices differ, so you have to do the sums yourself. Also, Whillans measured whether people were happier after buying time, not how many hours were saved.

### 10. Set a hard limit on short videos and aimless scrolling
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 给短视频和无目的刷屏设硬上限 | 5baba6a3 -->
- Cost: Free. The action takes a few minutes: turn off notifications, delete apps, or set a daily time limit on apps. The hard part is keeping your hands off and resisting opening them.
- In plain words: People in Japan spend about 184 minutes a day online on weekdays and about 193 minutes on days off, already more than watching TV. The largest single use of online time is watching video-sharing services. Cut an hour a day and you gain 365 hours a year.
- Benefit: The 2025 survey by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications' Institute for Information and Communications Policy (ages 13 to 79, diary-style records): average daily internet use across all ages was 183.9 minutes on weekdays and 192.9 minutes on days off; watching TV (live) was 156.1 minutes on weekdays and 189.2 minutes on days off. On both weekdays and days off, internet use exceeded TV, and the gap is widening. Among online uses, "watching video-sharing services" was the longest on both weekdays and days off. Among video-sharing services, YouTube's usage rate was 81.5% and TikTok's 36.7%. Cutting one hour of this a day adds 365 hours a year
- Evidence: C
- Sources: 総務省情報通信政策研究所 (2026). 令和７年度情報通信メディアの利用時間と情報行動に関する調査報告書（概要）. <https://www.soumu.go.jp/main_content/001079136.pdf>
- Notes: Online time includes work, study and socializing, not all of it wasted. Graded C because no study has calculated "how much is saved after limiting it, and what that time is used for"; there are only aggregate statistics. For the "seeing how others are doing" kind of scrolling, a randomized trial has measured time saved and mood change; see Section 24, entry 6 (do not make "how others are doing" daily reading).

### 11. Send an agenda before meetings, cancel meetings with no agenda, and stand up when you can
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 会前发议程，没议程的会不开；能站着开就站着开 | 78f49a9d -->
- Cost: Free. The organizer spends 5 to 10 minutes writing an agenda before the meeting. Standing meetings need participants' cooperation.
- In plain words: Meetings with an agenda sent beforehand are more likely to be seen as useful by participants. In an experiment, sitting meetings were 34% longer than standing ones, and the quality of decisions made did not differ between the two.
- Benefit: Two surveys with 958 and 292 people found that meetings with an agenda sent beforehand were more likely to be seen as effective by participants, a statistically robust relationship. In another experiment, 55 groups met sitting and 56 standing, and sitting meetings took 34% longer. The quality of decisions did not differ between the two formats.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Leach, D. J., Rogelberg, S. G., Warr, P. B., & Burnfield, J. L. (2009). Perceived meeting effectiveness: The role of design characteristics. Journal of Business and Psychology, 24(1), 65–76. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-009-9092-6>；Bluedorn, A. C., Turban, D. B., & Love, M. S. (1999). The effects of stand-up and sit-down meeting formats on meeting outcomes. Journal of Applied Psychology, 84(2). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.84.2.277>
- Notes: The standing meeting experiment used tasks for groups of 5 in a lab. The conclusion cannot be applied directly to long or large meetings. Leach measured how effective participants felt meetings were, not how much work was actually done.

### 12. Break big tasks into subtasks before estimating and starting
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 把大任务拆成子任务再估时、再开工 | 7098bdd5 -->
- Cost: Free. Breaking it down takes 5 to 15 minutes.
- In plain words: When estimating how long something will take, people do not automatically break the task down and count each part. Listing the subtasks before estimating reduces underestimation. Once broken down, each small step can be started right away, so it is less easy to put off.
- Benefit: When estimating, people do not themselves break a multi-part task into pieces. For example, writing a literature review, writing the discussion and sorting references are really several jobs. Listing subtasks before estimating reduces the tendency to underestimate, which is called the planning fallacy. Once broken down, each small step becomes an action that can be started immediately, and procrastination falls too. Among the strongest predictors of procrastination in research are aversion to the task and the task being pushed into the future.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Kruger, J., & Evans, M. (2004). If you don't want to be late, enumerate: Unpacking reduces the planning fallacy. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 40(5), 586–598. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2003.11.001>；Steel, P. (2007). The nature of procrastination: A meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential self-regulatory failure. Psychological Bulletin, 133(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65>
- Notes: How far to break it down: aim for "each subtask can be finished in one sitting"; breaking it down too finely itself takes time. Also, the Kruger and Evans study was only checked for direction from the abstract; the specific percentages were not checked against the original.

### 13. Put what you need within reach and move what you want to avoid further away, rather than counting on resisting in the moment
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 把要用的东西摆到手边，把不想碰的挪远，别指望当场忍住 | 76c5a2fc -->
- Cost: Free, a few minutes once. Put your phone in another room, and lay out your books and computer on the desk.
- In plain words: When things are a little further away, people take a little less. Moving the same snacks further away clearly reduced how much was eaten, a medium-sized effect. Offering fewer options also clearly reduced how many people took them. These methods do not require you to resist on the spot and can be set up in a few minutes.
- Benefit: A Cochrane review including 24 randomized trials. Placing food further away reduced the amount eaten, standardized mean difference −0.60 (95% CI −0.84 to −0.36, the confidence range; 12 studies, 1,098 people, low-certainty evidence), a medium effect. Reducing the number of options reduced selection of that food more markedly, standardized mean difference −1.13 (95% CI −1.90 to −0.37, 3 studies, 154 people, low-certainty evidence). Regression analysis showed: the further away and the fewer the options, the stronger the effect.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Hollands GJ, Carter P, Anwer S, et al. (2019). Altering the availability or proximity of food, alcohol, and tobacco products to change their selection and consumption. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 9, CD012573. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD012573.pub3>
- Notes: Why grade B: all 24 studies in this review were about food, none about alcohol or tobacco. 14 were done in labs, all in high-income countries. So applying "move it away and you touch it less" to phones and games is an extrapolation with no direct trial. Keeping the phone out of sight has itself been tested directly; see Section 24, entry 7 (turn off non-essential notifications, and keep your phone out of sight while working).

### 14. When choosing where to live, put commute time first, and shorten your one-way commute
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=多 时间=中 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 选住处时把通勤时长放在前面，缩短单程通勤 | f38c6c91 -->
- Cost: High. You may have to pay higher rent or move to a smaller place. Moving itself also takes time.
- In plain words: The longer people commute, the worse they feel their lives are. This loss is not made up for in pay or housing. 30 minutes less each way saves 5 hours a week.
- Benefit: In Japan's 2021 Survey on Time Use and Leisure Activities by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications, people aged 10 and over spent 31 minutes a day commuting to work or school, averaged over a week (people who do not work or study are included in the denominator). German data following the same people over many years show that people with longer commutes have consistently lower subjective well-being (how well they feel their lives are going). And this loss was not compensated by income or housing. A review bringing many studies together also confirms that the longer the commute, the less satisfied people are with it, regardless of mode of transport. 30 minutes less each way saves about 5 hours a week.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Stutzer, A., & Frey, B. S. (2008). Stress that doesn't pay: The commuting paradox. Scandinavian Journal of Economics, 110(2), 339–366. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9442.2008.00542.x>；Chatterjee, K., et al. (2020). Commuting and wellbeing: A critical overview of the literature with implications for policy and future research. Transport Reviews, 40(1), 5–34. <https://doi.org/10.1080/01441647.2019.1649317>；総務省統計局 (2022). 令和３年社会生活基本調査 生活時間及び生活行動に関する結果 結果の要約. <https://www.stat.go.jp/data/shakai/2021/pdf/youyakua.pdf>
- Notes: Disputed: Chatterjee and colleagues, pooling many studies, point out that no stable relationship between commute length and overall life satisfaction can yet be seen. They argue that people can broadly trade long commutes for better jobs or housing. Note also that 31 minutes is an average that includes people who do not commute; people who actually commute every day take much longer. And if you can travel off-peak, the same journey can take less time.

### 15. When deciding whether to continue, look only at future investment and future returns, not at how much you have already put in
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 决定是否继续时，只看未来投入和未来回报，不看已投入多少 | c61c7530 -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is accepting that the time already invested is gone and cannot be recovered.
- In plain words: Money and time already invested make people want to keep going. Pooling 98 sets of data, this tendency is consistent. So when deciding whether to continue, count only how much more you will put in and how much you will get back. Leave out what has already been spent.
- Benefit: Money, effort and time already invested make people more inclined to continue. Psychology calls this the sunk cost effect. Pooling 98 sets of data, the analysis confirmed the tendency is consistent. Avoiding it means you will not keep pouring in time just to "avoid waste".
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Arkes, H. R., & Blumer, C. (1985). The psychology of sunk cost. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 35, 124–140. <https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-5978(85)90049-4>；Roth, S., Robbert, T., & Straus, L. (2015). On the sunk-cost effect in economic decision-making: A meta-analytic review. Business Research, 8(1), 99–138. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s40685-014-0014-8>
- Notes: The Arkes and Blumer theatre experiment worked like this. Ohio University's theatre sold season tickets for 1982 to 1983. The researchers randomly gave the first 60 buyers one of three prices: full price of 15 dollars, 2 dollars off, or 7 dollars off. In the first half of the season, the full-price group attended 4.11 plays on average, the other two 3.32 and 3.29. In the second half, there was no clear difference among the three. Roth and colleagues found the effect fades over time and is smaller in older people.

### 16. Treat procrastination as avoiding discomfort, not as a lack of effort
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 把拖延当成在躲开难受，不要当成不够努力 | 6a5fd316 -->
- Cost: Free. All that changes is the direction of your judgment: first ask what about this task makes you uncomfortable, then look for a solution.
- In plain words: Procrastination has little to do with laziness or personality. What best predicts whether someone procrastinates is how unpleasant the task itself is to them, whether they believe they can do it, and how easily they are pulled away by what is in front of them. So what helps is making the task less unpleasant and making the first step smaller, not berating yourself and forcing it.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis pooling 691 correlations found strong and consistent links with procrastination for: how aversive the task is, how far away the deadline is, self-efficacy (believing you can do it), impulsiveness, and conscientiousness and its facets of self-control, distractibility, organization and achievement motivation. Neuroticism, rebelliousness and sensation-seeking were only weakly linked to procrastination. The author explains these results with temporal motivation theory: a task's present appeal shrinks rapidly as its reward becomes more distant.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Steel P (2007). The nature of procrastination: a meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential self-regulatory failure. Psychological Bulletin, 133(1), 65–94. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65>
- Notes: The benefit is rated medium because these studies only point a direction and did not measure how many hours are saved. Specific actions are in entry 1 of this section (write it as "at what time, where, and on what cue"), entry 12 (break big tasks into subtasks) and entry 5 (set a date for things with no external deadline). This pools correlational studies and cannot show what causes what.

### 17. Do not make highlighting, rereading or summarizing your main study methods
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 别把划重点、反复读、写摘要当成主要学法 | e12daad9 -->
- Cost: Free, and it saves time. The hard part is that these are the easiest to do and give the strongest feeling of "I'm studying".
- In plain words: Students most often use highlighting and rereading, yet in a ranking of ten common study methods both are in the lowest tier. Summarizing, the keyword mnemonic and imagining pictures for text are also in the lowest tier. They are not completely useless, but the conditions under which they work are too narrow, and the same time spent on self-testing is better value.
- Benefit: A review assessing ten common learning techniques rated them on "whether the effect generalizes across learning conditions, student characteristics, materials and assessment formats". Rated in the top tier were practice testing and distributed practice. In the middle tier were elaborative interrogation, self-explanation and interleaved practice. The five in the lowest tier were: summarization, highlighting (or underlining), the keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, and rereading. The review states: most students say they use rereading and highlighting, but these do not reliably improve performance and should be replaced by other methods, for example practice testing instead of rereading.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Dunlosky J, Rawson KA, Marsh EJ, Nathan MJ, Willingham DT (2013). Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques: Promising Directions From Cognitive and Educational Psychology. Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14(1), 4–58. <https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266>
- Notes: Underlining can be used to "mark what to self-test on later"; the problem is treating underlining itself as studying. The review also says the lowest tier does not mean zero, only that the conditions under which it works are narrow and more research is needed. For replacement actions, see entry 7 of this section (close the book and test yourself) and entry 8 of this section (spread the same time over several days).

### 18. Mix several types of problem when practising, rather than doing twenty of one type in a row
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 几种题型混着练，别一种连做二十道 | 7781360a -->
- Cost: Free. Instead of working through a workbook in order of problem type, shuffle them. The hard part is that mixed practice gives a lower score on the spot and feels like going backwards.
- In plain words: Doing a long string of the same type of problem feels smooth at the time, but afterwards you may not be able to tell which method to use. Practising several types shuffled together works better in the long run, by a medium amount. But it depends on the material: it works best for images and classification, less for maths problems, and for vocabulary blocked practice is actually better.
- Benefit: A multilevel meta-analysis including 59 studies, 238 effect sizes and 158 samples. The overall effect of interleaved compared with blocked practice was medium (Hedges' g = 0.42). By material: paintings were best (g = 0.67), and other visual materials also did well; maths tasks had a smaller effect (g = 0.34); expository texts and taste materials had no significant overall effect; for vocabulary, blocked practice was actually better (g = −0.39). Regression analysis showed: the more similar the categories to each other, the less similar items within a category, and the more complex the material, the greater the benefit of interleaving.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Brunmair M, Richter T (2019). Similarity matters: A meta-analysis of interleaved learning and its moderators. Psychological Bulletin, 145(11), 1029–1052. <https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209>
- Notes: The review cited in entry 17 of this section (do not make highlighting your main study method) rated interleaved practice in the middle tier, because there was not yet enough research at the time. Taking the two together, use it like this. Content where you need to "recognize which kind this is and which method applies" should be practised mixed, such as maths problem types, clinical cases and applying legal provisions. Content that must be memorized should be practised in blocks, such as vocabulary and definitions.

### 19. Test yourself on material to be memorized in the form you will need to use it, rather than just reciting it once as written
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 要背的内容按将来的用法出题考自己，别原样背一遍就算完 | 344a577f -->
- Cost: Free, and no extra time. Only the practice changes: replace "recite it once as written" with "set yourself questions in the form you'll use it later". The hard part is first thinking clearly about how you will use it.
- In plain words: Closing the book and testing yourself does not only help with the exact words you memorized. Pooling 192 comparisons and over ten thousand people, it also helps with questions in a different format, and with application and reasoning questions, by a small-to-medium amount. But it barely helps in two cases: swapping question and answer around, and content you only saw in the book and were never tested on.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis pooled over 40 years of research, with 67 published and unpublished papers, 122 experiments and 192 transfer effect sizes, 10,382 people in total. Compared with "restudying without testing", learning from practice testing did transfer to other contexts, effect size d=0.40 (95% CI 0.31 to 0.50). Transfer was strongest when: tested in a different question format, tested on application and inference questions, medical diagnosis problems, and tested on cue words with similar meaning. Transfer was weakest when: stimulus and response were swapped, material seen during initial learning but not tested, and worked-example problems. Among moderators, whether the response format matched between practice and test, whether retrieval practice came with detailed feedback, and whether the initial test was answered correctly all strongly affected whether positive transfer occurred. The authors also corrected for publication bias in two ways; effect sizes of moderators changed little, but the intercept estimate was clearly reduced: when none of the above conditions were present, positive transfer was often not seen
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Pan SC, Rickard TC (2018). Transfer of test-enhanced learning: meta-analytic review and synthesis. Psychological Bulletin, 144(7), 710-756. <https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151>
- Notes: The word "memorize" usually mixes two things: going over the material again (rereading, highlighting) and actively recalling with the book closed. The former is rated lowest in the review ranking ten study methods; for the ratings themselves, see entry 17 of this section (do not make highlighting, rereading or summarizing your main study methods). What to do is the latter: close the book and actively recall; for specifics see entry 7 of this section (close the book and test yourself), and do it over several days; see entry 8 of this section (spread over several days). The method is to first think through how you will use it later: picking it out on a test paper, or judging it on the spot, then set yourself questions in that form. Testing yourself the other way round is also worth doing, because in this meta-analysis transfer in the swapped direction was weakest, so both directions need practice.

### 20. To make yourself follow through, put money on the line, but first ask yourself whether you would actually stake it
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 想让自己做到就把钱押上，但先问自己肯不肯押 | 88c50261 -->
- Cost: A deposit scheme requires putting up some of your own money first, forfeited if you fail. In the trial the deposit was 150 dollars.
- In plain words: To push yourself to follow through, you can get a bonus when you succeed, or pay a deposit first and get it back plus a bonus when you succeed. Among people willing to join either, those paying a deposit succeeded 13 percentage points more often. But only 13.7% were willing to pay a deposit, versus 90% for a bonus, so a deposit only helps people who are willing to stake it.
- Benefit: A US randomized trial of 2,538 people randomly assigned employees and their friends and family to four incentive programs or usual care. Acceptance was 90.0% for the reward-based program (about 800 dollars for success) and 13.7% for the deposit-based program (150 dollars deposited first, plus a 650-dollar reward). Sustained smoking abstinence at six months: 9.4% to 16.0% across the four incentive programs, 6.0% with usual care. 15.7% for reward-based, 10.2% for deposit-based. But counting only the 13.7% "willing to join either", the deposit-based six-month abstinence rate was 13.2 percentage points higher than reward-based (95% CI 3.1 to 22.8, the confidence range).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Halpern SD, French B, Small DS, et al. (2015). Randomized trial of four financial-incentive programs for smoking cessation. New England Journal of Medicine, 372(22), 2108–2117. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1414293>
- Notes: This trial tested quitting smoking; applying it to writing a thesis or working out is an extrapolation, so the benefit is rated medium. When choosing a scheme, first consider whether you would actually use it. A method most people would not accept, however strong, does nothing for them. So pick the kind you are willing to accept; staking a little is better than staking nothing. For how to quit smoking itself, see Section 20, entry 14 (smoking cessation medicines).

### 21. Count new habits in months, not "21 days"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 新习惯按月算，不要按「21 天」算 | 72b626bd -->
- Cost: Free. What it takes is repeating the same action in the same setting every day for several months.
- In plain words: "21 days to form a habit" has no basis. A study tracking people for 12 weeks found that for an action to become automatic took 18 days for the quickest and 254 days for the slowest. Not finding it easier after two or three weeks is normal. Missing a day does not reset your progress either.
- Benefit: 96 volunteers each chose an eating, drinking or activity behaviour to do every day in a fixed setting (such as "after breakfast") for 12 weeks, rating their automaticity daily. 82 people's data were sufficient for analysis, and the model fitted successfully for 62. The number of days to reach 95% of each person's automaticity plateau ranged from 18 to 254. The study also found that missing one opportunity to perform the behaviour did not materially affect the habit formation process.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Lally P, van Jaarsveld CHM, Potts HWW, Wardle J (2010). How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world. European Journal of Social Psychology, 40(6), 998–1009. <https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674>
- Notes: There is only this one study, 96 people, all doing simple actions (such as eating a piece of fruit after a meal). How long complex things take, such as running three times a week or writing every day, has not been measured. So still finding it hard after two weeks is not evidence of failure. To make the repetition actually happen, tie the action to a fixed setting; see entry 1 of this section (write it as "at what time, where, and on what cue").

### 22. If procrastination already affects your life, use self-help materials supported by randomized trials; you do not necessarily need a therapist
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 拖延已经影响生活的，用有随机试验支持的自助材料，不用非找治疗师 | 533b8f11 -->
- Cost: A self-help book is cheap, and some are free. It takes about ten weeks, reading one section and doing one exercise a week.
- In plain words: Procrastination severe enough to cause distress can be treated. Following cognitive behavioural self-help materials for ten weeks, whether guided by someone or done entirely alone, was clearly better than doing nothing. Guided and unguided showed no difference, so doing it on your own also works.
- Benefit: 150 people were randomized into three groups: therapist-guided self-help, unguided self-help, and a waiting-list control. After ten weeks, between-group effect sizes on the Pure Procrastination Scale were d = 0.70 for the guided group (95% CI 0.29–1.10, the confidence range) and d = 0.50 for the unguided group (0.10–0.90); on the Irrational Procrastination Scale, 0.81 (0.40–1.22) and 0.69 (0.29–1.09). Proportions reaching clinically significant improvement: 31.3% to 40.0% in the guided group and 24.0% to 36.0% in the unguided group. The two groups did not differ significantly on any outcome.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Rozental A, Forsell E, Svensson A, Andersson G, Carlbring P (2015). Internet-based cognitive-behavior therapy for procrastination: A randomized controlled trial. Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 83(4), 808–824. <https://doi.org/10.1037/ccp0000023>
- Notes: Participants were Swedish adults using Swedish-language materials; there is no ready-made equivalent set in Chinese, so the benefit is rated medium. The materials' components are the usual ones: behavioural activation, behavioural experiments, stimulus control, and explanations of motivation and work methods. Stimulus control is in entry 13 of this section (move what you want to avoid further away). If procrastination comes with clear low mood or anxiety, first follow Section 24, entry 5 (move, get sunlight, sleep on schedule, talk to someone).

### 23. Spend time once learning keyboard shortcuts and automation for software you use often
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 花一次时间学常用软件的键盘快捷键和自动化 | 84adaf30 -->
- Cost: Free. It takes a one-off investment of a few hours to a few dozen hours of learning. The hard part is that at first it is slower than using the mouse.
- In plain words: Most people who have used Word for years still click toolbar icons. In comparison experiments, shortcuts were faster than both menus and icons. Learning is a one-off cost, and every operation every day after that pays it back. The longer you do the same kind of work, the more you gain.
- Benefit: The study looked at 251 experienced Word users; most rarely used the faster shortcuts and mainly clicked icon toolbars. Comparison experiments confirmed that of menus, icons and shortcuts, shortcuts were fastest. Time saved accumulates with the number of operations each day. People doing the same kind of work for a long time gain most.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Lane, D. M., Napier, H. A., Peres, S. C., & Sándor, A. (2005). Hidden costs of graphical user interfaces: Failure to make the transition from menus and icon toolbars to keyboard shortcuts. International Journal of Human-Computer Interaction. <https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327590ijhc1802_1>
- Notes: Learn only the operations you use ten or more times a day; learning them all is another kind of waste. Note that the efficiency experiment had only 6 participants, and the abstract does not give the seconds saved per operation.

### 24. Do not choose study methods by "I'm a visual learner, they're an auditory learner"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=时间 -->
<!-- zh: 别按「我是视觉型、他是听觉型」挑学法 | 94f30d5a -->
- Cost: Free. Simply stop choosing materials and courses by this classification, and stop paying for learning style assessments.
- In plain words: Many people believe everyone has their own learning style, such as visual or auditory, and learns better when taught that way. Very few studies have tested this properly, almost none support it, and several found the opposite. People do have preferences, but there is no evidence that teaching to them produces better learning, so there is no need to pay for a learning style assessment.
- Benefit: A review commissioned to assess the evidence on learning styles states: to show that "teaching to learning styles" works, students must first be grouped by style, then each group randomly assigned to different teaching methods, then all tested with the same test. The criterion for effectiveness is a specific interaction: the method most effective for one style is not the most effective for another. The review concluded that this interaction was almost never found; although the literature on learning styles is large, very few studies used a proper experimental design, and several of those directly contradicted the "meshing hypothesis". On this basis the authors judged that the current evidence does not support using learning style assessments in educational practice.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Pashler H, McDaniel M, Rohrer D, Bjork R (2008). Learning Styles: Concepts and Evidence. Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 9(3), 105–119. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x>
- Notes: The review also explains that this does not mean every version of learning style theory has been tested and refuted; many have never been tested at all. What to avoid is paying for style assessments and choosing training courses by style.
